### 1. Eigenvalues (Characteristic Roots) & Eigenvectors

> 🟢 **Definition:** Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ square matrix. A scalar $\lambda$ is called an **Eigenvalue** (or characteristic root / latent root) of $A$ if there exists a **non-zero** column vector $X \ne 0$ such that:
> $$\mathbf{A X = \lambda X \iff (A - \lambda I) X = 0}$$
> The non-zero vector $X$ is called the **Eigenvector** (or characteristic vector) corresponding to $\lambda$.

#### Characteristic Equation & Matrix Invariants (3×3 Shortcut):
For $A X = \lambda X$ to have a non-trivial solution $X \ne 0$, the matrix $(A - \lambda I)$ must be singular:
$$\mathbf{|A - \lambda I| = 0}$$
For a $3 \times 3$ matrix $A$, the characteristic polynomial expands directly using trace and principal minors:
$$\mathbf{\lambda^3 - S_1 \lambda^2 + S_2 \lambda - |A| = 0}$$
Where:
- **$S_1 = \text{Tr}(A) = a_{11} + a_{22} + a_{33} = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3$** *(Sum of main diagonal elements = Sum of eigenvalues)*
- **$S_2 = M_{11} + M_{22} + M_{33}$** *(Sum of cofactors/minors of main diagonal elements)*
- **$|A| = \det(A) = \lambda_1 \cdot \lambda_2 \cdot \lambda_3$** *(Determinant = Product of eigenvalues)*

#### Key Properties of Eigenvalues:
1. The eigenvalues of an **upper or lower triangular (or diagonal) matrix** are simply its main diagonal elements.
2. The eigenvalues of a **real symmetric matrix** ($A^T = A$) are always **purely real**, and eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are mutually **orthogonal** ($X_i^T X_j = 0$).
3. If $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$, then:
   - $\lambda^k$ is an eigenvalue of $A^k$.
   - $\frac{1}{\lambda}$ is an eigenvalue of $A^{-1}$ (for $|A| \ne 0$).
   - $\frac{|A|}{\lambda}$ is an eigenvalue of $\text{adj}(A)$.

### 2. Cayley-Hamilton Theorem & Its Applications

> 🟢 **Theorem Statement:** **"Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation."**
> If the characteristic equation of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is:
> $$|A - \lambda I| = (-1)^n \left( \lambda^n + c_1 \lambda^{n-1} + c_2 \lambda^{n-2} + \dots + c_n \right) = 0$$
> Then replacing scalar $\lambda$ by matrix $A$ and constant $c_n$ by $c_n I$ yields the zero matrix:
> $$\mathbf{A^n + c_1 A^{n-1} + c_2 A^{n-2} + \dots + c_n I = 0}$$

#### Two Primary Applications of Cayley-Hamilton Theorem:
1. **Finding Matrix Inverse $A^{-1}$ (when $c_n = (-1)^n |A| \ne 0$):**
   Multiply the matrix equation by $A^{-1}$ and isolate $A^{-1}$:
   $$\mathbf{A^{-1} = -\frac{1}{c_n} \left( A^{n-1} + c_1 A^{n-2} + \dots + c_{n-1} I \right)}$$
2. **Computing Higher Matrix Powers ($A^4, A^8$, etc.):**
   Express $A^n$ in terms of lower powers $(A^{n-1}, \dots, A, I)$ and substitute iteratively.

**University Question (6 Marks - MMDU Annual Exam):**
For matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 3 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$: (1) Verify Cayley-Hamilton Theorem, (2) Evaluate $A^{-1}$, and (3) Compute $A^4$.

---

**Step 1 🚀: Characteristic Equation via Invariants $S_1, S_2, |A|$**
- $S_1 = \text{Tr}(A) = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3$
- $S_2 = M_{11} + M_{22} + M_{33} = (1 - 1) + (1 - 3) + (1 - 0) = 0 - 2 + 1 = -1$
- $|A| = 1(0) - 2(3) + 1(-3) = -9$
- Characteristic equation: $\lambda^3 - 3\lambda^2 - \lambda + 9 = 0$.

**Step 2 ⚡: Verify $A^3 - 3A^2 - A + 9I = 0$**
$$A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 & 0 \\ -3 & 2 & -2 \\ 6 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}, \quad A^3 = A^2 \cdot A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 11 & 1 \\ -9 & -2 & -7 \\ 21 & 11 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$$
Substituting into $A^3 - 3A^2 - A + 9I$:
$$\begin{bmatrix} 4-12-1+9 & 11-9-2+0 & 1-0-1+0 \\ -9+9-0+0 & -2-6-1+9 & -7+6+1+0 \\ 21-18-3+0 & 11-12+1+0 & 7-15-1+9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{Verified!})$$

**Step 3 🎯: Deduce $A^{-1}$ and $A^4$**
- Multiply by $A^{-1}$: $A^2 - 3A - I + 9A^{-1} = 0 \implies$
  $$A^{-1} = -\frac{1}{9}(A^2 - 3A - I) = \frac{1}{9}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 & -1 \\ 3 & -7 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$$
- Multiply by $A$: $A^4 = 3A^3 + A^2 - 9A$.
